Two questions were raised that the previous state of the project couldn't answer: how does the chatbot's knowledge base actually work, and what were the design choices and results behind the risk model. Both questions were fair — at the time, there was no system to point to. That's now been built. This brief walks through both, in plain terms, with the technical specifics attached for the record.

Question 1
How does the chatbot's knowledge base work?

What it actually knows

The assistant draws from a small, deliberately curated library of medical content — not the open internet, and not the AI's general training knowledge. The library covers what cervical cancer is, its symptoms, prevention, the HPV vaccine, screening schedules, risk factors, and how to interpret test results — sourced from WHO, CDC, and American Cancer Society patient guidance. Keeping it narrow and vetted, rather than broad and scraped, was the deliberate choice: in a health app, getting less wrong matters more than covering more ground.

How it finds the right answer

When a user asks something, the system doesn't send the question straight to an AI and hope for the best. It first searches the library for the passages most relevant to that specific question, then hands only those passages to the AI and instructs it to answer strictly from them. If nothing in the library is relevant enough, the assistant says so honestly rather than guessing.

Staying on topic

Anything unrelated to cervical cancer or reproductive health — weather, politics, entertainment — is redirected before it ever reaches the AI. The assistant is scoped on purpose; it isn't meant to be a general chatbot.

Question 2
What are the risk model's design choices — and what are its results?

Why it isn't a trained prediction model

There is a public research dataset this kind of tool is often built on — but it only covers 858 people, with very few confirmed cancer cases and a lot of missing answers. Training a predictive model on data that thin tends to produce results that look precise but aren't actually reliable — a real risk in a health context. So instead of training a model to guess, the system was built to reason transparently from established medical risk factors.

How a score is actually produced

Each answer a user gives is checked against a set of known risk factors — prior test results, HPV or HIV status, smoking history, screening history, and others — and contributes points according to how strongly that factor is linked to cervical cancer risk in medical guidance. The points add up to a score, which maps to a Low, Moderate, or High result.

Why there's no "1 in 12" style statistic

An early option was showing a precise-sounding figure — something like "you have a 1 in 12 chance." That was deliberately rejected. The system has no data connecting its point total to real-world incidence rates, so a number like that would look far more certain than it actually is. Instead, the result gives a clear risk level and the specific reasons behind it — accurate to what the system actually knows.

What "results" means for a system like this

Because nothing was trained on data, there's no accuracy percentage to report the way there would be for a machine-learning model — and that's a stated design decision, not a gap. Every completed assessment is saved, on purpose, so that if the weighting ever needs to be tuned against real usage patterns, the data to do that already exists.
